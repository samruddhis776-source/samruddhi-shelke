## 1. Introduction

An **Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR)** is a harmful and unintended response to a medicine when it is used at normal doses for prevention, diagnosis, or treatment of a disease.

Examples of possible ADRs include skin rash, itching, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, and swelling. These symptoms can also be caused by an illness or another factor, so the patient's history and the timing of symptoms must be reviewed before linking them to a medicine.

**Important difference:** An adverse event is any unwanted medical occurrence after a medicine is used; it does not automatically mean the medicine caused it. An ADR is a harmful response for which a causal relationship with the medicine is suspected.

## 2. Objective

- Review the patient's symptoms and medical history.
- Check all medicines taken, including dose and start date.
- Compare the time the medicine was started with the time symptoms appeared.
- Identify a suspected medicine when the available evidence supports suspicion.
- Record missing information and assess causality carefully.

## 3. Steps for identifying a possible ADR

1. **Review the patient:** Note age, sex, medical conditions, allergies, and relevant history.
2. **Describe the reaction:** Record each symptom, its severity, onset date, duration, and outcome.
3. **Review medication history:** Include prescription medicines, over-the-counter medicines, herbal products, supplements, dose, route, and start/stop dates.
4. **Check the timeline:** Did the reaction begin after the medicine was started or its dose was increased? Is the timing medically plausible?
5. **Consider other causes:** Check the underlying disease, infection, food, other medicines, and other possible explanations.
6. **Review withdrawal or re-exposure information:** Did symptoms improve after the suspected medicine was stopped (dechallenge)? Did they return after it was restarted (rechallenge)? Rechallenge should not be performed just to prove causality, especially after a serious reaction.
7. **Assess causality:** Use the method required by the organization, such as WHO–UMC or the Naranjo scale, if sufficient information is available.
8. **Document and report:** Record facts clearly and report suspected ADRs through the appropriate pharmacovigilance process. Do not independently stop or change a patient's treatment; clinical decisions should be made by a qualified healthcare professional.

## 5. Practice case scenarios

The following are **illustrative training examples**, not confirmed real patient records.

### Case 1 — Rash and itching after a new medicine

**Scenario:** A patient starts Medicine A. Two days later, the patient develops a new rash and itching. No other new medicine is reported. The patient contacts a healthcare professional and is advised to get assessed.

- **Possible reaction:** Skin rash and itching.
- **Suspected medicine:** Medicine A, as a possible suspect.
- **Reasoning:** The symptoms began after the medicine was started, so the timeline supports a possible association.
- **Information still needed:** Exact medicine and dose, allergy history, other causes, clinical assessment, and outcome.
- **Conclusion:** A possible ADR should be investigated; causality cannot be confirmed from this information alone.
### Case 2 — Nausea after a dose increase

**Scenario:** A patient has been taking Medicine B for several weeks. The dose is increased. Nausea begins the following day.

- **Possible reaction:** Nausea.
- **Suspected medicine:** Medicine B, particularly the recent dose increase.
- **Reasoning:** The symptom followed a change in dose, which may support a relationship.
- **Information still needed:** Dose details, other medicines, illness history, severity, and whether symptoms improve after a clinician changes treatment.
- **Conclusion:** A possible dose-related ADR should be considered, while other causes are assessed.

### Case 3 — Dizziness while taking multiple medicines

**Scenario:** An older patient taking Medicines C and D reports dizziness. The start dates and doses are not available.

- **Possible reaction:** Dizziness.
- **Suspected medicine:** Cannot be selected confidently between Medicines C and D.
- **Reasoning:** More than one medicine may be relevant, and the timeline is missing.
- **Information still needed:** Full medication list, doses, start dates, blood pressure where clinically appropriate, other symptoms, and medical history.
- **Conclusion:** This is an adverse event requiring assessment; the cause is undetermined with the information provided.

### Case 4 — Symptoms improve after a medicine is stopped

**Scenario:** A patient develops a symptom after starting Medicine E. A healthcare professional stops or changes the medicine, and the symptom later improves. No rechallenge is performed.

- **Possible reaction:** The reported symptom (to be specified in a real report).
- **Suspected medicine:** Medicine E.
- **Reasoning:** Improvement after withdrawal (positive dechallenge) can support a causal relationship, but does not prove it by itself.
- **Information still needed:** Exact dates, other treatment, natural course of the condition, and alternative causes.
- **Conclusion:** The information may support an association; a formal causality category requires the complete case details.

## 6. Causality assessment

Causality assessment estimates how likely it is that a medicine caused a reaction. It is not always possible to prove causation.

Common approaches include:

- **WHO–UMC:** Categories commonly include Certain, Probable/Likely, Possible, Unlikely, Conditional/Unclassified, and Unassessable/Unclassifiable.
- **Naranjo scale:** A structured questionnaire that assigns points based on factors such as timing, alternative causes, dechallenge, rechallenge, and previous evidence.

A causality score or category should **not** be assigned when key information is missing. Never invent dates, medicine names, test results, or patient outcomes to complete a report.

## 7. Information to collect in an ADR case

- Patient details relevant to the assessment (avoid unnecessary identifying information).
- Description of the reaction and date/time of onset.
- Suspected medicine(s): name, dose, route, indication, start and stop dates.
- Concomitant medicines, supplements, and herbal products.
- Relevant medical history, allergies, and other possible causes.
- Treatment or action taken and patient outcome.
- Dechallenge/rechallenge information, if it occurred as part of clinical care.
- Reporter details and required reporting documentation, according to local procedures.

## 8. Key learning points

1. A symptom occurring after medicine use does not automatically prove an ADR.
2. The timeline between medicine exposure and symptom onset is important.
3. A complete medication history helps identify the possible suspect drug.
4. Alternative explanations must be considered.
5. Serious or unexpected suspected reactions should be documented and reported according to applicable procedures.
6. Patient safety comes first; treatment changes must be handled by a qualified healthcare professional.
7. Missing information should be recorded as unknown rather than guessed.

## 9. Conclusion

ADR identification involves reviewing symptoms, medication history, timing, alternative causes, and the patient's outcome. The aim is to identify a possible relationship between a medicine and a harmful reaction while avoiding unsupported conclusions.
